\section{Utility Evalution of Downstream Tasks}\label{sec:utility}
Most market generator research, particularly in this sector, are
primarly interested in the statsitical similarities while
ovelook experimenting on downstream effects of synthetic
market data in practical settings \cite{Horvath2025, Stenger24}.
While many works consider downstream scenarios such as option delta
(deep) hedging \cite{Tanaka25,Boursin22}, core time series generation
benchmakr such as TSGBench fail to incorporate such experiments due to
the various industries that Ang et. al aimed to benchmark.

We propose three (3) experiments to demostrate the downstream effects of
synthetic market data through a train-synthetic-test-real (TSTR) framework
to replicate realistic application of synthetic market data taht financial 
instituitions and market participants will use \cite{Horvath2025, Stenger24}. 
In addition to the utility of option deep hedging, a popular utility benchmark 
among market generator papers \cite{Tanaka25,Boursin22},
for the first time in the literature, we introduce the application of risk 
management/portfolio hedging from the perspective of an equity market maker, 
and portfolio construction/alpha generation from the persective of a 
hedge fund manager or proprietary trader.

\subsection{Overview of Utility Evaluation}
    The general framework of utility evalution is structured
    the same across our three downstream tasks, centering around the 
    train-synthetic-test-real (TSTR) evaluation framework 
    \cite{Leznik21} with a rolling-window validation for hyperparameter tuning, 
    adapted for financial time series 
    generation. We begin with our real market dataset 
      part partition as specified in \ref{subsec:data-preprocess}
      \textemdash along with a specific learning task \(\mathcal{T}\) and a 
      performance score \(S\) to evaluate task effectiveness.
      We consider an algorithm \(A\) (e.g. a 5-layer 
      perceptron) that takes as input the dataset \(D\) and 
      task \(\mathcal T\).

      In the context of market generator evaluation, we consider a market 
      generator \( G_{TS} \) trained on \( D^{train} \) 
      and validated with \( D_r^{validate} \) to generate synthetic 
      FTS data \( D_g \).

      Now we can train a algorithm \( A \) on the 
      datasets \( D_r^{train} \) and \( D_g^{train} \) 
      respectively, resulting in two models
      \( M_r \) and \( M_g \).

      Finally, we evaluate both models on the real test set 
      \( D_r^{test} \) to obtain the corresponding performance 
      scores \( s_r \) and \( s_g \).

      The goal of the (deep) hedger portfolio evaluation is to 
      evaluate the effectiveness of the model 
      \( \mathcal{M}_{TS} \) with downstream tasks (hedging) 
      by comparing the scores \( s_r \) and \( s_g \). The model 
      \( \mathcal{M}_{TS} \) is considered effective if the 
      the results yielded similar scores, i.e. 
      \( s_r \approx s_g \) \textemdash preferably having higher 
      performance with results of \( s_g > s_r \) \textemdash 
      indicating that the synthetic data generated by 
      \( \mathcal{M}_{TS} \) is useful for training a (deep) hedger.

      \subsubsection{Augmented Testing}
      \label{sec:augmented-testing}
      To better evaluate the performance of (deep) hedgers trained 
      on synthetic data, we train our (deep) hedger \( A \) on a 
      new dataset \( D_{aug} \) that combines both real training 
      data \( D_r^{train} \) and synthetic data \( D_g\). In 
      other words, we train model \( M_g \) on \( D_{aug} \) 
      where \( D_{aug} := D_r^{train} \cup D_g\).
      Then we proceed to evaluate scores between models
      \( M_g \) and \( M_r \) on the real test set, where 
      \( M_r \) is still trained on only real data 
      \( D_r^{train} \).
      This extension, inspired by Stenger et al. \cite{Stenger24}, 
      allows us to assess whether synthetic data provides additional 
      value when combined with real training data.


      \subsubsection{Algorithm Comparison}
      \label{sec:algorithm-comparison}
      Another extension is to compare multiple (deep) hedger 
      algorithms to determine the degree to which each algorithm 
      performs equally on generated data relative to other algorithms, 
      compared to their performance on real data.

      Given a set of \( n \) (deep) hedger algorithms 
      \( \mathcal{A}_n = \{ A_1, \ldots, A_n \} \), we train 
      each algorithm \( A_i \in \mathcal{A}_n \) on both real 
      training data \( D_r^{train} \) and synthetic data 
      \( D_g^{train} \) respectively, resulting in two sets of 
      (deep) hedger models \(\{M_r^1, M_r^2, \ldots, M_r^n\} \) 
      and \( \{M_g^1, M_g^2, \ldots, M_g^n\} \) and two ordered 
      sets of performance scores 
      \( s_r := \{s_r^1, s_r^2, \ldots, s_r^n\} \) and 
      \( s_g := \{s_g^1, s_g^2, \ldots, s_g^n\} \) respectively.

      We compare the relative performance of each 
      algorithm on real data versus synthetic data by computing 
      the Spearman's rank correlation coefficient \( r_S \) 
      \cite{Lin21} between the two score sets \( s_r \) and 
      \( s_g \):
      \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
          r_S &= 1 - \frac{6 \sum_{i=1}^{n} ( \mathrm{rank}(s_r^i) 
          - \mathrm{rank}(s_g^i) )^2}{n(n^2 - 1)},
        \end{aligned}
        \label{eq:Spearman}
      \end{equation}
      where \( \mathrm{rank}(s_r^i) \) and 
      \( \mathrm{rank}(s_g^i) \) denote the ranks of scores 
      \( s_r^i \) and \( s_g^i \) within their respective sets.

      A high positive correlation (i.e., \( r_S \) 
      close to 1) indicates that the synthetic data 
      generated by \( \mathcal{M}_{TS} \) preserves the relative 
      performance of different (deep) hedger algorithms, suggesting 
      that the synthetic data is suitable for algorithm evaluation 
      and comparison.

      The (deep) hedger algorithms \( \mathcal{A}_n \) considered in 
      this benchmark can be found in appendix 
      \ref{appendix:deep-hedger-algorithms}.

\subsection{Option Delta Hedging}
Option hedging is employed by options market making trading desks to minimize the downside 
counter-party risk when a call expires or a put is exercised. By the two
fundamental theorem of asset pricing, the option contract can be priced and
replicated using the underlying security and the money market. For this utility
evaluation, we take a perspective of an options market maker selling a
Europan call option of a cash equity at strike $K$ and maturity $T$.


\subsection{Portfolio Hedging}
    Portfolio hedging is a common practice among equity market makers 
    to mitigate the risk of adverse price movements in their inventory. 
    In this utility evaluation, we simulate a market maker's inventory 
    management strategy using synthetic market data generated by the 
    market generator $ G $. The market maker aims to maintain a 
    balanced portfolio by dynamically adjusting their positions in 
    response to market fluctuations.

    Classical market-making models assume that dealers can reduce inventory 
    risk through an external hedging venue once inventory becomes sufficiently large
    \cite{Gueant2011}. 
    To mitigate aggregate market and sector risk, we assume the market
    maker can partially hedge accumulated stock inventory using broad-market 
    and sector ETFs. This reflects common industry practice of using highly 
    liquid index-tracking instruments as temporary hedges before inventory is 
    fully unwound. While classical market-making models generally represent 
    hedging via a generic external market rather than a specific instrument, 
    our implementation instantiates this external hedge using liquid U.S. 
    ETFs (see table \ref{tab:asset-classification}),
    which serve as liquid proxies for market and sector exposures.

    \subsubsection{Portfolio Hedging Definition}
    For a time horizon \( T \) and a universe of $K$ assets, we define the
    market maker's inventory
    at time \( t \) as \( I_t\coloneq (I_{t,1}, \ldots, I_{t,K})\in \mathbb{R}^K \).
    Inventory positions are
    modeled as continuous exposures rather than integer share counts, consistent 
    with the portfolio-level nature of the daily hedging problem.

    Given a hedging model \( M \) trained on market data and a market generator \( P \)
    that produces synthetic price trajectories, the portfolio hedging task is to find
    a set of hedge positions \( H = \{h_{t,j}\}_{t=1,j=1}^{T,J} \) in $J=5$ liquid ETFs
    that minimize the notional exposure of the inventory portfolio netted against the
    ETF hedge portfolio as follows. Formally, the objective is to solve the
    following optimization problem:
    \begin{equation}
      \begin{aligned}
        \min_H \sum_{t=1}^{T-1}  \left\vert \sum_{i=1}^K x_{t+1,i}I_{t,i} - \sum_{j=1}^{J} \tilde{x}_{t+1,j}h_{t,j}\right\vert\quad 
      \end{aligned}
      \label{eq:portfolio-hedging-objective}
    \end{equation}
    where $x_{t,i}$ and $\tilde{x}_{t,j}$ are the daily log returns of the inventory
    and ETF per share, respectively. The hedging model \( M \) learns to
    dynamically adjust the hedge positions across the five ETFs to offset inventory
    risk and maintain a market-neutral stance.

    \subsubsection{Inventory Simulation}
    Since proprietary inventory records are unavailable, we model 
    inventory as a stationary first-order autogressive process. 
    This choice is motivated by the inventory-risk framework of Guéant et al.,
    where optimal quoting strategies continuously adjust bid and ask quotes
    to reduce inventory exposure toward a target level \cite{Gueant2011}. 
    Rather than explicitly simulating the market-making strategy and order arrivals, 
    we adopt a reduced-form approximation that preserves the primary stylized fact 
    of dealer inventories: mean reversion toward a risk-neutral inventory level.
    
    We consider a mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process for each asset \( i \):
    \begin{equation}
        I_t = \alpha I_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha^2} \Sigma^{1/2}\epsilon_t
    \end{equation}
    where \( \alpha \in (0,1) \) is the ``persistence'' parameter of the inventory
    process, \( \Sigma^{1/2} \) is the Cholesky decomposition of the sample covariance 
    matrix of the inventory shocks, and \( \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}_K(0, I) \) 
    is a standard K-dimensional Gaussian white noise.

    \subsection{Alpha Generation}
