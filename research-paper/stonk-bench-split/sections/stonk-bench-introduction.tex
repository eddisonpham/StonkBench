\section{Introduction}
  Synthetic market data generation has emerged as a crucial tool in
  financial markets, particularly time series
  of financial asset returns, trading volume, and limit order books
  \cite{Potluru24, Horvath2025}, emerged from the rising popularity 
  of generative models in other domains over the past decade. 
  The ability to generate high-quality
  synthetic financial data addresses several critical challenges in
  the field, like simulating fat-tail behaviors and volatility clustering
  observed in financial returns time series and developing
  back-testing procedures for prediction and (deep) hedging
  algorithms \cite{Stenger24}.

  Despite the growing importance of market generators,
  there is a notable lack of standardization in evaluating the 
  quality and effectiveness of these generative models; 
  \textit{Stenger et al.} surveyed 56 research papers on time series
  data and concluded that there are no concensus in the
  evaluation metrics and their interpretations.
  \cite{Stenger24}. Current evaluation 
  methods vary widely across studies, making it difficult to 
  compare different approaches objectively and determine their 
  relative strengths and weaknesses.

  \subsection{A brief overview on market generators}
    Synthetic market data are (multivariate) time series related to financial
    assets and market microstructures; such examples are limit order books, trading volume,
    volatility surfaces and, as the focus of this paper, asset returns \cite{Assefa2019, Stenger24, Horvath2025}.
    We refer to synthetic generated data for financial asset returns time series and its data generator 
    as simply synthetic market data and market generator, respectively, from now on. 
    There are generally two primary reasons \cite{Horvath2025} why academic 
    researchers and practitioners are interested in generating synthetic market data:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{Forecasting and Prediction:} Synthetic market data can generate future market paths
      to used for training
      and evaluation of forecasting models, enabling researchers to test their algorithms on a 
      wide range of scenarios. With recent advances in deep learning, generative models 
      like GANs and VAEs, particularly their conditional variants, gives more desireable results
      as they can learn conditional distributions of financial time series data as opposed to 
      point estimators \cite{Horvath2025}.
      \item \textbf{Simulating Market Scenarios:} The ability to generate synthetic market 
      data allows researchers and practitioners to simulate various market scenarios alternativly
      from historical data, 
      including extreme events and rare occurrences. This is particularly valuable for 
      stress testing trading strategies, risk management models, and financial algorithms 
      under different market conditions. It comes to no surpise as other domains like computer 
      vision and natural language processing have seen similar trends in the use of 
      synthetic data for training and testing models \cite{Wang20, Lu25}. However, financial
      market data is inherently more challenging due to its non-stationary, time-dependent nature, 
      on top of the fact that there is only "one realization" of the market to which the market
      generator wishes to learn the distribution from. Market generators that captures historical
      data distributions can act as data augmentation
      to improve backtesting robustness and reduce overfitting to historical data \cite{Horvath2025}.
    \end{enumerate}
  We refer to Horvath et al. \cite{Horvath2025} for a more comprehensive historical overview of 
  market generators and their applications in financial research and practice.

  \subsection{Limitations in the market generator Literature}
    Among other things, we observed that there is currently no 
      universal or generally accepted approach to evaluating synthetic 
      time series; this issue extends beyond sythetic market generator
      to generative frameworks like GANs as a whole \cite{Wang20}
      and time series generation \cite{Stenger24}. 
      Many evaluation measures are insufficiently defined and lack 
      public implementations, making reuse and reproduction 
      troublesome and error-prone \cite{Stenger24}. This presents a 
      challenge unique to the generation task compared to areas like 
      time series forecasting or classification \cite{Stenger24}. 
      Hence, future research would immensely benefit from a widely 
      accepted, reasonably sized set of qualified measures for the 
      central evaluation criteria. Here we outline observations on 
      the current limitations in market generator research and evaluation 
      practices:
      
      \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{[L\arabic*]}]
        \item \label{limitation:metrics}
        \textbf{Inconsistent Evaluation Metrics:} Different 
        studies employ varying metrics, hindering direct comparisons 
        between models. For instance, some focus on statistical 
        similarity, while others emphasize utility in downstream tasks 
        \cite{Stenger24}.
        
        \item \textbf{Lack of Standardized Datasets and 
        Preprocessing:} The absence of common benchmark datasets leads 
        to evaluations on disparate data, making it challenging to 
        assess model performance uniformly \cite{Stenger24} in terms 
        of both asset classes, data granularity, and data length. 
        Furthermore, preprocessing steps (e.g., normalization, 
        windowing) vary widely, affecting results and complicating 
        comparisons.
        
        \item \textbf{Narrow Scope of Evaluation:} Many evaluations 
        concentrate on a narrow set of criteria, such as marginal 
        distributions, neglecting other important aspects like 
        temporal dependencies, generation diversity, and downstream
        tasks
        \cite{Stenger24, Horvath2025}.
        
        \item \textbf{Reproducibility Issues:} The lack of 
        open-source implementations and detailed evaluation protocols 
        impedes reproducibility and validation of results across 
        different studies \cite{Stenger24,Potluru24}. It is important
        to note that most model papers did not provide code for their 
        evaluations.
      \end{enumerate}

  \subsection{Our Contributions}
  In this paper, we developed a unified market generator for historical  financial time series on 
  equity returns by adopting the time series evaluation
  taxonomy outlined from Stenger et al. \cite{Stenger24}, implementing the time series
  generation framework from Ang et al. \cite{Ang23}, and extending the utility
  evaluation frameworks implimented by Boursin et al.and
  outlined by Fecamp et al. \cite{Boursin22, Fecamp20} (from option pricing deep hedging
  to portfolio hedging and alpha generation). We also investigate the various
  potential trade-offs between different evaluation metrics and categories of metrics.
  Our key contributions include:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{[C\arabic*]}]
    \item \label{contribute:1} \textbf{Consolidated Evaluation
      Framework:}
      In effort to address \ref{limitation:metrics}, we systematically
      review and consolidate evaluation methods from leading papers
      (models and surveys) in market generators
      \cite{Stenger24, Ang23, Boursin22} and create a comprehensive
      assessment toolkit based on established practices.

    \item \label{contribute:2} \textbf{Unified Statistical and
      Utility Measures:}
      For the first time, we integrate both statistical fidelity metrics
      and practical utility measures in a single benchmark,
      providing a more complete evaluation of synthetic data quality.

    \item \label{contribute:3} \textbf{Outline Benchmark Framework:}
      We deliver an open-source benchmark framework to share with the
      research community, aiming to establish cohesion and standard for
      future market generator research in evaluation and comparison.
  \end{enumerate}

  \subsection{Research Questions}
  Our benchmark aims to address the following research questions:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{[Q\arabic*]}]
    \item \label{rq:1} \textbf{How do different sequence lengths during
      training affect market generator performance?}
      We investigate the impact of varying training sequence lengths on
      the performance of deep learning market generators as an
      extension of existing work like Allen et al. \cite{Allen2024}.
      The selected training sequence lengths are described in 
      §\ref{sec:model-specific}.

    \item \label{rq:2} \textbf{Is there a trade-off between certain
      metrics or categories of metrics?}
      In an effort to incorporate an ensemble of evaluation metrics, we
      are interested in understanding potential trade-offs between these
      metrics and how they impact the overall performance synthetic market data
      \cite{Ang23}. Particularly, we are interested in a market generator's 
      ability to balance fidelity and diversity as highlighted in
      recent studies \cite{Lu25}. More importanly, we pay special care
      to the statistical quality of a market generator versus its utility when
      augmenting downstream task performance.

    \item \label{rq:3} \textbf{Whether new deep learning models
      actually outperform classical parametric models in downstream tasks?}
     Most existing literature focuses on deep learning-based methods tend 
     to dominate the field and claims their superiority in capturing complex
     market data distributions \cite{Horvath2025,Tanaka25,Takahashi24,Assefa2019}. 
     We aim to provide a comprehensive comparison 
     between deep learning-based approaches and traditional methods, focusing on
      their respective strengths and weaknesses in generating high-quality
      market data and its utility in downstream tasks.

      \end{enumerate}
